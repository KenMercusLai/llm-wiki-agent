# 太原 / Taiyuan (Warring States)

## Overview
Taiyuan in this page is the Warring States/Qin northern strategic area and commandery, with a later Chu–Han reuse of its name.

## Current Profile
The episodes describe a transition from Qin’s 259 BCE post-Changping attack route to a commandery seat at Jinyang in the 247 BCE Qin narrative and then a later Han commandery/campaign setting. Administrative extent and control cannot be assumed continuous across reigns.

## Key Characteristics
- Post-Changping Taiyuan is a Qin attack target tied to Zhao, Shangdang and the route toward Handan.
- In the Qin Zhuangxiang Wang third-year account it becomes a newly established Qin commandery, not merely a battlefield target.
- Jinyang’s subsequent unrest during Ying Zheng’s succession qualifies any impression of secure Qin control.
- After Han Xin’s defeat of Wei Bao, Taiyuan is reused as a Han administrative and northern campaigning base; this is a later polity, not uninterrupted jurisdiction.

## Evidence
- **A campaign target preceded a commandery.** After the [[ChangpingBattle]], the 259 BCE narrative assigns [[SimaGeng]] the [[TaiyuanWarringStates|Taiyuan]]/[[Shangdang]] attack while [[WangHe]] strikes [[WuanWarringStates]] and [[Pilao]], widening [[QinState]] pressure on [[ZhaoState]] toward [[Handan]]. The episode contrasts [[ZizhiTongjian]]’s two-route account with [[Shiji]]’s three-route variant; neither makes Taiyuan an already-secure Qin commandery at this stage. [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-95-changping-zhi-zhan-hou-qinguo-weishenme-meiyou-mie-le-zhaoguo-1-lq4wvkd8qx3q7zji6fnnkxi3qcl1]]
- **Qin’s administrative claim followed continued conquest.** In the 247 BCE third-year account of [[QinZhuangxiangwang]], Qin takes previously unsubmitted Shangdang towns and first establishes Taiyuan commandery amid [[MengAo]]’s attack on Wei and wider [[QinEastwardPressure]]; the source also reports fourteen Qin commanderies in total. The later establishment notice is not another rendering of the 259 BCE raid. [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-103-1-simaqian-kanzhong-de-xinlingjun-suan-shi-xiaoren-ma-lvty1pdnibvqpk0m16kkxzw4t-ck]]
- **The seat remained contested after succession.** The continuation identifies [[Jinyang]] as the new commandery seat, then recounts revolt after Qin Zhuangxiang Wang died and thirteen-year-old [[QinShiHuang]] succeeded under [[LuBuwei]]’s regency. A commandery name therefore should not imply settled local control. [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-103-2-simaqiande-idol-weihe-siyu-jiuse-lvmqg1ojmka9pqagfhgvo4albpjx]]
- **Chu–Han reuse refers to a new polity and campaigning base.** After [[HanXin]] captured [[WeiBao]], Han divided former Wei territory into [[HedongCommandery]], Shangdang and Taiyuan commanderies; Han Xin then defeated Dai forces around [[EyuBattle]], took Jinyang and used the north as a route toward Zhao. The source relates both the Wei incorporation and subsequent Jinyang capture to Taiyuan’s setup, but does not establish continuous Qin-to-Han borders or uninterrupted jurisdiction. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-155-2-zhuan-gei-ni-piqi-da-de-pengyou-weiwang-panhan-jing-shi-liubang-ai-maren-ls5nvunmhakvefd2oxn4zdyoob4l]]

## Qualifications
“太原” denotes a region/administrative name at distinct times; the 259 BCE battlefield and later Qin/Han commanderies must not be collapsed into one fixed boundary. The Zhouji source records divergent two-route/three-route traditions; the Hanji note itself relates both Wei-territory incorporation and the later Jinyang conquest to Taiyuan’s establishment.

## What Changed
- The military target and later Qin and Han commanderies are treated as distinct stages, not a continuous jurisdiction.
- Jinyang’s revolt and variant campaign-route traditions qualify a smooth conquest narrative.
