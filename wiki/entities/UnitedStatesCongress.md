## Overview
The United States Congress appears here as the legislature that controls federal appropriations, delegates monetary authority, sets boundaries on temporary tariff powers and can scrutinize executive data use. Podcast accounts distinguish legislative powers from specific proposals and survey respondents' wishes.

## Current Profile
Across the registered sources, Congress is a bargaining arena during a government shutdown and a possible—not automatic—guardrail for central-bank independence, trade authority, climate-project funding and federal privacy accountability.

## Key Characteristics
- Budget bargaining can interrupt government operations and the timely economic data on which policy depends.
- Congress's statutory delegation to the [[FederalReserve]] protects rate decisions from day-to-day voting but leaves contested removal and confirmation questions.
- Temporary executive tariff authority and selective appropriations require legislative decisions rather than unlimited executive discretion.
- Congressional oversight is a requested remedy for federal-data mistrust, not an investigation documented in the polling episode.

## Evidence
- **Budget and monetary delegation:** A November 2025 discussion treats the [[AffordableCareAct]] subsidy shutdown standoff as [[PoliticalVetoPointBargaining]] that delayed statistics needed by the [[FederalReserve]]; its speakers interpret congressional delegation of difficult rate decisions partly as blame avoidance, alongside specialist judgment, not a recorded vote on each rate. [[146-meiguo-jingji-zheme-cha-meigu-hai-neng-jixu-zhang-ma-chuantai-meilun-meihuan-lrshkxgofprel-e-7lkiqu-en8gu]] [[LaelBrainard]] separately proposes that Congress define [[ForCauseRemovalStandard]] with grounds such as corruption or negligence; Senator Tom Tillis's reported use of [[JeromePowell]] inquiry concerns in [[KevinWarsh]]'s prospective chair confirmation illustrates a potential check on [[DonaldTrump]]'s pressure, not enacted reform. [[jerome-powell-and-the-test-of-fed-independence]]
- **Limits on tariff power and selective funding:** After the [[SupremeCourt]] rejected broad IEPA tariffs, [[KathleenClaussen]] explains [[Section122TariffAuthority]] as a temporary balance-of-payments tool capped at 15% and 150 days unless Congress extends it—not prior legislative permission for every tariff. [[the-supreme-court-struck-down-a-bunch-of-trumps-tariffs-now-what]] [[AaronBurns]] of [[Carbon180]] says Congress protected planned [[DirectAirCapture]] hubs in [[Louisiana]] and [[Texas]], including the [[OccidentalPetroleum]]-backed project, despite other rescissions; this is selective [[CarbonRemovalPoliticalDurability]], not the survival of all grants. [[tech-20260713-tech-pod-128-tech-20260713-tech-pod-128]]
- **Demand for data oversight:** [[ElizabethLaird]]'s polling finds concern about federal personal-data practices and sharing with law enforcement or DHS; nearly half said uncertain handling could deter benefit sign-ups. Respondents wanted accountability from bodies such as Congress, so [[CenterForDemocracyAndTechnology]]'s [[GovernmentDataAccountability]] and [[FederalDataPracticeTrust]] discussion describes a requested oversight channel, not an opened investigation. ## Qualifications
The shutdown and rate-delegation motives are speakers' interpretation. Brainard's suggested removal grounds and Tillis's reported confirmation stance are not enacted Fed rules. Carbon-removal protection applies to selected hubs, not the entire program; the reported statutory Section 122 limit does not imply Congress must approve every tariff before its temporary imposition. Public polling indicates desired oversight, not completed congressional action.

The planned carbon-removal hubs illustrate [[CarbonRemovalPoliticalDurability]] only for the specifically protected Louisiana and Texas projects, not the sector as a whole. [[tech-20260713-tech-pod-128-tech-20260713-tech-pod-128]]
