# Chokepoint Shipping Confidence

## Definition
Chokepoint shipping confidence is the willingness of shipowners, cargo owners and insurers to use a contested waterway, distinct from its formal legal or military declaration of openness.

## Current Synthesis
The cited 2026 [[Iran]]–[[UnitedStates]] [[StraitOfHormuz|Hormuz]] reports connect attack and possible mine risk, disputed passage authority, insurance prices, toll allegations and inventories to actual commerce. A passage can be nominally open but commercially avoided; conversely, particular ships may pass under uncertain terms. The accounts are date- and speaker-specific, not a settled continuous history of the war.

## Key Claims
- Insurance and owner decisions can transmit military threat into freight and energy prices before a total physical closure.
- Vague safe-passage language leaves room for incompatible notions of free transit and discretionary permission.
- Alleged tolling and cargo-loss allocation add contractual and payment uncertainty, even where a particular shipment survives.
- A governance deal requires acceptable control rules; a proposed no-toll consortium cannot solve the problem if inbound authority remains disputed.
- Producer output or quota changes cannot alone restore usable oil supply while passage and downstream stocks are constrained.

[[GregCarlstrom]] cited three attacked ships, July 12 traffic of 11 versus earlier 30–50, and oil rising from about $71 to the mid-$80s. These are an episode snapshot, not evergreen baseline statistics.
- [[how-to-get-through-the-strait-of-hormuz]] follows [[PlanetMoney]]’s account of [[ChristianStClair]] mistakenly believing that [[FantagraphicsBooks]] cargo was aboard attacked [[OneMajesty]]; it was still in India, later on another pink ship. [[RyanPetersen]] explains [[GeneralAverage|shared voyage-loss exposure]] and the larger [[FreedomOfNavigationTradeOrder|freedom-of-navigation]] concern; do not treat the toll as verified universal policy.
- [[omission-accomplished-why-the-iran-war-cycle-spins-on-6a6c7475aa1642a30f17f1cb]] says [[Oman]] proposed a roughly ten-day truce and regional joint management without mandatory tolls, while Iran demanded complete control of inbound Gulf traffic. Its July 31 assessment reports fighting resumed after roughly three weeks of a thin June memorandum; this is a later failed-management account, not a hypothetical successful consortium.
- [[the-secret-meeting-that-launched-opec]] presents a source-scoped account of the [[UnitedArabEmirates|UAE]] leaving [[OPEC]] and seeking higher output. Its near-term price caveat is about [[StraitOfHormuz]] traffic and replenishment of reserves, tankers, terminals and refinery stocks: formal production capacity is not immediately deliverable supply.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- The five sources report different moments and speaker judgments in 2026. Neither formal closure claims nor transit by one ship settle market-wide safety; reported strikes, traffic and tolls have distinct evidentiary status.
- Some passages may reopen or be rerouted; an oil-price movement in the cited episode does not establish a single-cause estimate. Tolls could be borne as another cost, but discretionary access is the larger risk in the logistics interview.

## What Changed
- Cargo-owner confusion and alleged payment administration sharpen the distinction between formal passage and trusted passage.
- A later no-toll consortium proposal failed over control; producer-side supply cannot bypass that confidence constraint.
