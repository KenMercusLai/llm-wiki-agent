# 州牧集权与地方割据 / Provincial Shepherd Autonomy

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-940-de-minxin-zhe-de-tianxia-ni-xin-bu-xin-loipf7b0p5a-yh0rj2njnsml8a1j|Hanji 940]] supplies the entry crisis behind Liu Yan's later autonomy. [[MaXiangLateHan|马相]] and [[ZhaoZhiLateHan|赵祗]] expose the weakness of ordinary provincial authority in [[YizhouLateHan|益州]], [[JiaLongLateHan|贾龙]] restores temporary order, and [[LiuYanLateHan|刘焉]] enters through [[YizhouPacificationByLeniency|益州宽惠安抚]] before his rule hardens into the more coercive route-control structure developed in Hanji 959.

州牧集权与地方割据 / Provincial shepherd autonomy enters the wiki through [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-959-ruguo-ni-chuanyue-dao-sanguo-gai-ruhe-cunhuo-lroaihmpnrelmfcugym9zx2h2dhq|Hanji 959]] as the institutional hinge behind [[LiuYanLateHan|刘焉]]'s [[YizhouLateHan|益州]] rule. The episode says Liu Yan had proposed replacing weaker provincial inspectors with stronger provincial shepherds because crisis conditions required officials who could gather military and administrative resources.

The source treats the reform as double-edged. More concentrated provincial authority can stabilize a region when the center is weak, but it also gives the provincial ruler enough coercive, fiscal, and personnel power to become difficult for the court to restrain. Liu Yan's Yizhou is the concrete case: he kills local strongmen, blocks routes toward Chang'an, retains [[LiuZhang|刘璋]], and moves from self-protection toward signs of autonomous rule.

This concept differs from ordinary "regional geography" pages. It names the institutional mechanism that lets geography, armies, roads, and titles fuse into a local regime.

## Key Claims

- Hanji 940 adds that provincial autonomy can begin as a plausible answer to local revolt and absent authority before it becomes separatist structure.
- Strengthening provincial office can solve short-term disorder while weakening central control.
- A provincial ruler's autonomy becomes durable when office, troops, road control, and local intimidation reinforce one another.
- The late-Han court's residual legitimacy remains real, but it becomes harder to enforce once provincial governors can physically block communication.
- Liu Yan's Yizhou case helps explain fragmentation before the later succession problems around [[LiuZhang|刘璋]].
