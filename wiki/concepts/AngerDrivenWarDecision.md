# Anger-Driven War Decision / 因怒兴师

## Definition
Anger-driven war decision is the substitution of grievance repair for a defensible political aim and a workable military plan. The issue is not whether a leader feels anger or has a genuine grievance, but whether that feeling chooses the timing, target, and risk of force.

## Current Synthesis
The historical episodes describe distinct decision points: a ruler escalating after deception, a commander planning an attack amid a disputed claim, and a subordinate abandoning a defensive order under provocation. Their common failure is to let humiliation outrank attainable interests, public justification, and operational exposure. A trusted intervention can interrupt an order, while restraint before an insult is a useful analogy; neither alone proves a stable strategy.

## Key Claims
- A real injury does not make retaliatory war strategically sound when the objective is poorly chosen.
- After an initial defeat, continued focus on humiliation can deepen losses by ignoring recoverable interests and logistics.
- Anger can bypass political settlement and a usable public pretext before the first attack.
- Tactical taunts can overturn a sound defensive assignment and expose an army at a vulnerable crossing.
- Interest-based restraint and trusted mediation can interrupt escalation, but a cancelled attack is not a completed strategy.

## Evidence
### Claim 1 — grievance versus objective
- Zhang Yi's promised six hundred li becomes only six li after Chu has broken with Qi. The episode portrays Chu Huaiwang's attack on Qin as a response to deception and humiliation, while invoking Sunzi's warning against raising troops in anger. [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-51-chuhuaiwang-qin-xiao-er-kan-jiujiu-ruhe-mie-le-ni-ltfkdlivomnvux5xodh2rlfqf6ac]]

### Claim 2 — escalation and exposure
- After Danyang and the loss of Hanzhong, the same account says Chu mobilizes again toward the previously promised Shangyu rather than prioritizing the land just lost. The advance toward Lantian stretches supply and exposes Chu to Han and Wei pressure in its rear. These are the episode's narrated campaign sequence and strategic judgment. [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-51-chuhuaiwang-qin-xiao-er-kan-jiujiu-ruhe-mie-le-ni-ltfkdlivomnvux5xodh2rlfqf6ac]]

### Claim 3 — attack without settlement design
- In the pre-Hongmen account, Liu Bang's pass closure and Cao Wushang's report about Guanzhong sharpen Xiang Yu's genuine dispute. Xiang Yu orders an attack; the host faults the absence of a demand-and-refusal sequence or plan for governing the outcome, even while crediting Fan Zeng with identifying Liu Bang as a threat. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-140-xiangyu-yu-liubang-shi-zhen-youqing-haishi-xianghu-liyong-1-lmmhoq2wo3bs6b1johpc0ixuf662]]

### Claim 4 — provocation at Chenggao
- Xiang Yu instructed Cao Jiu to hold Chenggao and avoid battle. Han insults drew Cao Jiu out; Han forces attacked Chu mid-crossing at Sishui, and the source records the loss of Chenggao and the Aocang grain hub. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-163-liubang-shouxia-moushi-ruhe-bei-hanxin-neijuan-cansi-lubinu6hoihu59knmlixmaryhnq7]]

### Claim 5 — interruption and threshold
- Xiang Bo invokes kinship, Liu Bang's prior merit, and the charge of injustice to get Xiang Yu to cancel the next-morning assault. Separately, the Han Xin biography uses the interest-gated Sunzi rule and Han Xin's refusal of a street fight as an analogy for not spending a future on immediate shame repair. The latter is not itself a war decision. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-140-xiangyu-yu-liubang-shi-zhen-youqing-haishi-xianghu-liyong-2-lvdgirxmwxcckh2loh-yk2zzer2e]] [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-145-junshi-qicai-hanxin-bolan-zhuangkuo-de-yisheng-2-npgife56ltayww2wt4wo-fiwo-ep]]

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- Xiang Yu's claim to Guanzhong was genuinely contested, and Fan Zeng saw a plausible strategic threat. The criticism is the episode's assessment of the unprepared political sequence, not proof that all force against Liu Bang would have been irrational. Xiang Bo's intervention is expedient and factionally situated, not proof of a durable peace policy. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-140-xiangyu-yu-liubang-shi-zhen-youqing-haishi-xianghu-liyong-1-lmmhoq2wo3bs6b1johpc0ixuf662]] [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-140-xiangyu-yu-liubang-shi-zhen-youqing-haishi-xianghu-liyong-2-lvdgirxmwxcckh2loh-yk2zzer2e]]
- Han Xin's humiliation story is a personal-scale analogy, not evidence that he made a particular war decision; the episode's proposed family lineage and inherited military education remain conjectural. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-145-junshi-qicai-hanxin-bolan-zhuangkuo-de-yisheng-2-npgife56ltayww2wt4wo-fiwo-ep]]
- The numbers and campaign chain in the Chu account are source-level narration rather than independent verification. The Cao Jiu example concerns violation of an existing defensive command rather than a ruler's decision to initiate a war. [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-51-chuhuaiwang-qin-xiao-er-kan-jiujiu-ruhe-mie-le-ni-ltfkdlivomnvux5xodh2rlfqf6ac]] [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-163-liubang-shouxia-moushi-ruhe-bei-hanxin-neijuan-cansi-lubinu6hoihu59knmlixmaryhnq7]]

## What Changed
- Consolidated state-level escalation, pre-attack political sequencing, and battlefield command failure as distinct mechanisms.
- Made the defensive alternative and lost recovery opportunity in the Chu case explicit.
- Distinguished actual war choices from Han Xin's personal restraint analogy and an attack order's temporary cancellation.
