# Affection-Driven Succession Risk / 宠爱驱动的立储风险

## Definition
Affection-driven succession risk arises when a ruler's preference for a partner or child changes an established heir arrangement without resolving rival claims, authority or protective coalitions. It differs from [[SuccessionNonDesignationRisk|leaving an heir unnamed]]: a formal designation can itself create a divided polity.

## Current Synthesis
The Zhao Wuling Wang, later Zhao and early Han episodes illustrate different outcomes, not one inevitable collapse. Preference may produce a completed heir change, a ruler who abdicates but retains command, or a failed attempt that still exposes the favored alternative branch.

## Key Claims
- A favored younger son's installation can convert family preference into institutional and factional competition with an elder branch.
- Formal abdication does not settle succession when the former ruler retains command and competing authority remains legible.
- Compensation through territory and advisers may equip the displaced branch for conflict instead of reconciling it.
- Rival mobilization can exploit ambiguous summons and court loyalties, but the violence is a particular outcome rather than a universal succession law.
- A threatened but uncompleted heir change can expose both the incumbent and alternative heir when the latter lacks an elite coalition or durable protection.
- Favorite-driven replacement without retained former-ruler power is a distinct risk that must be assessed with external military pressures, not treated as a single cause of defeat.

## Evidence
- **Preference and formal transfer:** [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-57-gudai-fa-dushi-haiyou-zhe-zuoyong-lj6wi61osaytowgmdp8dtkrwb-si]] introduces [[ZhaoWulingwang|Zhao Wuling Wang]]'s marriage to [[WuWa]] and younger [[ZhaoHe]] alongside elder [[ZhaoZhang]]; this is a foreshadowing, not evidence of the later coup. [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-61-shui-shi-lishishang-daner-zui-fei-de-diwang-lvpoaszanascnlrmu2atinlxzmk1]] records his 299 BCE transfer to ten-year-old Zhao He, [[FeiYi]]'s chancellorship and his own retained military role as 主父. His daring disguised Qin reconnaissance and northern anti-Qin strategy explain why the retained command had a substantive purpose, without excusing the [[PartialAbdicationPowerSplit|authority split]].
- **Provisioning a rival branch:** After conquering Zhongshan in 295 BCE, [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-66-zhongguo-lishishang-naxie-diwang-shi-bei-huohuo-e-si-de-1-lvl1uxjadiedqwad-skxrwf-usnq]] describes Zhao Zhang as Anyang Jun in Dai with [[TianBuli]], while [[LiDui]] warns Fei Yi of resentment and violent allies. The former ruler's discomfort at Zhao Zhang bowing to Zhao He prompts a proposal to divide Zhao; Zhao He rejects it. This is a [[PowerExitTrap|failed exit from authority]], not merely affection for Wu Wa.
- **Coup and liability:** [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-66-zhongguo-lishishang-naxie-diwang-shi-bei-huohuo-e-si-de-2-ltsw2eilkegs5yjtrp6gkhrmsxsg]] recounts a forged summons, Fei Yi's protective advance and death, [[GaoXinWarringStates|Gao Xin]]'s defense, and troops of Zhao Cheng and Li Dui suppressing Zhao Zhang at [[ShaqiuCrisis|Shaqiu]]. They kill Zhao Zhang and confine the old ruler without food for about three months rather than seek an explicit order from the young king: the source frames this as [[ResponsibilityAvoidantKilling|responsibility-avoidant killing]]. Zhao He's silence is the host's interpretation, not proof he ordered it.
- **Later Zhao variant:** [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-111-1-ruoda-de-zhaoguo-jing-wang-yu-yige-jinv-zhi-shou-lhrqb8-swlkuyoydgjyn8ldnlkzc]] says [[ZhaoDaoxiangwang]] displaced [[ZhaoJia]] for [[ZhaoYoumiuWang|Zhao Qian]] after favoring his mother. In 236 BCE Zhao's Yan campaign created an opening exploited by Qin forces; the episode's polemical title attributing Zhao's collapse to one woman exceeds what this source establishes. This is [[FavoriteDrivenHeirDisplacement|direct displacement]] without the Wuling Wang abdication structure.
- **Early Han failed change and protection:** [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-181-jiemi-liubang-feili-taizi-de-zhenshi-yuanyin-lgnbvnnfwxe4weupqgz-rh9cbp2s]] says [[LiuBang]] favored [[QiJi]] and [[LiuRuyi]] over [[LiuYing]] and [[LuZhi]]. [[ZhouChang]] refused a change and was sent as Zhao chancellor on [[ZhaoYao]]'s advice, which the host views as weak protection without central command: [[AlternativeHeirProtectionFailure|an exposed alternative heir]]. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-189-liubang-siqian-zuihou-yibo-lllydj2xyk4cr7b9d2qx0hvfw-dz]] records resistance by [[ShusunTong]] and the visible support of [[ShangshanSiHao]] for the crown prince; [[ZhangLiang]]'s avoidance was different from direct remonstrance. The replacement did not take place, though the preference persisted.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- The two Zhouji 66 installments narrate one unfolding crisis, not independent corroboration. The host's interpretations of intent, filial silence and familial causality should not replace evidence about commanders, ministers and the older ruler's institutional choices.
- Late Zhao's military exposure and accession overlap; the source does not establish a one-woman cause of conquest. Hanji 181's prediction of Liu Ruyi's vulnerability is an interpretation rather than proof of a particular later fate.
- The early Han change failed where the Wuling Wang transfer succeeded; birth-status rules such as [[BirthTabooSuccessionExclusion]] are adjacent institutional context, not the mechanism demonstrated by these episodes.

## What Changed
- Integrated the three dynastic cases by distinct succession mechanisms and outcomes rather than episode arrival.
- Located Zhao's catastrophe in divided command and armed rival provisioning, not affection alone.
- Distinguished early Han's weak alternative-heir protection from the incumbent heir's protective coalition.
