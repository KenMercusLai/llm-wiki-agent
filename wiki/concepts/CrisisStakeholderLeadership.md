## Definition
Crisis stakeholder leadership is rapid prioritization when a firm's survival or critical service is threatened and different affected groups cannot all be protected without cost.

## Current Synthesis
Three participant interviews locate responsibility at different levels: Airbnb's guests, hosts, workers and lenders; Gusto's customer payroll and employees downstream of those customers; and SVB depositors, regional banks and policymakers. Communication, liquidity and a clear account of who bears the loss matter more than claiming a frictionless win for every stakeholder.

## Key Claims
- Recognizing the scale of a crisis and establishing frequent decision and communication cycles can accelerate action.
- A stakeholder-first decision may injure another group, requiring explicit remediation rather than rhetorical harmony.
- Protecting cash and rebuilding an operating organization can coexist with painful financing and staffing choices.
- A payroll provider's responsibility may extend beyond its direct customer to employees waiting for wages.
- Ecosystem advocates can translate local bank distress into broader payroll and contagion terms, while admitting the moral-hazard cost of guarantees.

## Evidence
- Severity and cadence: [[socialradarspod-brianchesky2-final]] recounts [[BrianChesky]] saying [[Airbnb]] lost roughly 80% of business in eight weeks as COVID disrupted travel; board member [[KenChenault]] warned the shock could exceed 9/11 or 2008. Chesky describes daily executive standups, weekly all-hands Q&As, board updates and principles of speed, cash and stakeholders. [[tsr-s2-wrapup-v1]] is the same show's retrospective mention of possible bankruptcy and his resolve not to let COVID end Airbnb, not independent corroboration.
- Tradeoffs and repair: [[socialradarspod-brianchesky2-final]] says Airbnb refunded about $1 billion of guest deposits against hosts' cancellation expectations and then committed $250 million to host support. The account also describes $2 billion emergency debt instead of a heavily dilutive equity deal, cut initiatives and layoffs; [[TrustAsBusinessAsset]] here had a real balance-sheet and employment cost.
- Downstream continuity: [[tsr-s4-gusto-v3-tsr-s4-gusto-v3]] has [[JoshReeves]] report that nearly 10,000 [[Gusto]] customer companies banked with [[SiliconValleyBank]] in March 2023 and Gusto risked company capital to keep employees paid; multiple processors and a reliability-first payroll posture are relevant [[FinancialOperationsResilience]], not proof of a general legal obligation.
- Public response: [[tsr-s5-ronconway-v5-tsr-s5-ronconway-v5]] gives [[RonConway]] of [[SVAngel]]'s account of a roughly $42 billion run in eight hours after the March 8 loss disclosure, and his network's push to “guarantee deposits” before Tokyo markets opened. [[GarryTan]] and YC gathered payroll exposure; Conway describes contact with Treasury and political figures including [[NancyPelosi]] and [[KamalaHarris]]. He explicitly frames the [[DepositGuaranteeCrisisResponse]] as a [[MoralHazardContagionTradeoff]] rather than a cost-free rescue.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
All four notes come from [[TheSocialRadars]]; the wrap-up repeats Chesky's interview. Financial figures, Conway's influence on policy and Chesky's post-crisis morale and profitability are interview accounts, not independent audit findings. Protecting payroll or deposits in these cases cannot establish that every provider or government should assume the same financial risk.

## What Changed
- Separated company operating tradeoffs, infrastructure continuity and policy-level advocacy.
- Treated the season recap as a recap rather than an independent case.
