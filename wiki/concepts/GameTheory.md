# Game Theory

## Definition
[[GameTheory]] studies choices whose payoffs depend on other actors' choices, expectations and information, rather than on one's move alone.

## Current Synthesis
A penalty shootout illustrates near-simultaneous strategic choice and mixed strategy. Workplace contracts, bargaining and expert trust involve different structures—repetition, [[InformationAsymmetryInContracts|hidden information]] and signaling—and should not be treated as the same zero-sum model.

## Key Claims
- Repeated predictable play can make even a technically strong action exploitable.
- Preparation changes opponents' beliefs and can expose one's own pattern in turn.
- Workplace rules create cooperation or gaming incentives where effort and quality are difficult to observe.
- Bargainers can use alternatives, concessions and issue trades to change both information and the achievable agreement.
- Trust repair depends partly on costly, accountable signals rather than cheap declarations.

## Evidence
- Mixed strategy: Planet Money's account of Stefan Szymanski and Ignacio Palacio Huerta says a taker may need to use a weaker side sometimes to avoid predictable play; goalkeeper and taker often commit before observing the other's move. It presents professional penalties as a closer fit to mixed-strategy predictions than many lab games, not a universal rule for all play. [[how-to-win-a-penalty-shootout-with-game-theory]]
- Data and counteradaptation: Chelsea commissioned Palacio Huerta's analysis before the 2008 final against Manchester United. In the episode's telling, Petr Cech saved Cristiano Ronaldo's kick; Chelsea then repeatedly aimed to Edwin van der Sar's left, John Terry slipped, and van der Sar signaled awareness of that pattern before saving Nicolas Anelka's kick. Data helped but also made a repeated response legible. [[how-to-win-a-penalty-shootout-with-game-theory]] The Soccernomics discussion separately distinguishes overblame of ordinary-match penalties from shootout strategy; its note does not support the old Jens Lehmann/Ribery details. [[22-zuqiu-jingjixue-duzhe-bubi-shi-qiumi-570874427]]
- Incentive design: the discussion of Nie Huihua's [[YiQieJieQiyue|《一切皆契约》]] uses prisoner's dilemmas and tit-for-tat to explain repeated cooperation, publisher discount wars to show a collectively costly signal trap, and ratcheted targets or subjective appraisals to explain worker withholding and flattery. Degrees, commissions and equity can signal or align interests yet also distort them. [[79-gewei-lingdao-danfan-zan-xuedian-boyilun-690004262]]
- Negotiation: the discussion of 《哈佛经典谈判术》 links BATNA and [[ReservationValue|reservation value]] to a [[ZoneOfPossibleAgreement|zone of possible agreement]]; the first offer may anchor or reveal information, visible concessions convey value, and multi-issue or contingent terms can enlarge a deal under uncertainty. Listening for interests matters more than assuming a purely zero-sum contest. [[106-bie-zai-haipa-chongtu-kuai-lai-xuexi-tanpan-shu-742539645]]
- Credibility: Ben Ho proposes apology, transparency and costly signals of time, money or accountability to rebuild economists' trust after forecasting and distributional-policy mistakes. This is an interview proposal rather than measured proof of restored trust. [[would-you-trust-an-economist-with-your-economy]]

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Penalty tactics do not reduce to statistics: execution, pressure and opponent inference interact. The 2008 final is one narrated case, not a replicated causal test. The workplace and negotiation episodes discuss books, while Ho's account is interpretive; these illustrate different strategic mechanisms, not one interchangeable formal game.
