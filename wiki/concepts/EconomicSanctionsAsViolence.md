# Economic Sanctions As Violence

## Definition
“Economic sanctions as violence” is an ethical reading of coercive economic restrictions that asks whether civilian harm is hidden by the description “alternative to war”; it does not equate sanctions with armed attack or quantify deaths.

## Current Synthesis
Financial isolation, oil restrictions and frozen reserves reach households through prices, fuel, health services and investment even when policy formally targets officials. The Iran episode also records diplomatic leverage; Venezuela and Cuba show sanctions interacting with prior oil dependence, exchange controls, domestic decisions and pandemic disruption. Afghanistan raises the question of harm when pressure fails to moderate rulers. The moral frame belongs to the sources, not an independently measured causal attribution for every hardship.

## Key Claims
- Dollar-finance and energy pressure can raise civilian prices and obstruct medicine or basic services beyond the formally targeted actors.
- Humanitarian exemptions can fail to deliver transactions when banks and suppliers avoid even permitted business.
- Diplomatic leverage and civilian harm can coexist; concessions, insider gains and representative legitimacy must be assessed separately.
- Pre-existing domestic economic vulnerabilities make sanctions an additional mechanism, not the sole cause of a crisis.
- Withholding reserves, aid and recognition may leave a regime unmoved while deepening household and health-care distress.

## Evidence
- [[Iran|Iran]]'s channel: [[iran-protests-and-sanctions]] follows protester [[AliIranProtester|Ali's]] account of currency collapse, food-price pressure and bazaar protests. This is an attributed [[EconomicHardshipProtestTrigger|hardship-to-protest pathway]], not proof that sanctions alone caused the protests. Eva Leila Pesaran and Esfandyar Batmanghelidj explain oil/import pressure and [[DollarFinancialSanctions|global dollar-bank access]]; the 2015 nuclear agreement is a diplomatic result, but post-deal banks remained wary of doing business and the 2018 withdrawal undermined relief. The source also argues politically connected Revolutionary Guard insiders can gain while ordinary people lose access to necessities.
- [[Cuba|Cuba]]'s compound shocks: [[dark-times-for-cubas-economic-experiment]] follows Yaser González Cabrera's CityCleta [[TourismDependentSmallEconomy|tourism collapse]] in Havana and Ricardo Torres's account of the Soviet oil subsidy, [[Venezuela|Venezuela]]'s later oil role, constrained private reform, U.S. travel/oil pressure and the pandemic. The source's 1991 GDP decline of 35% predates the contemporary pressure; blackouts, fuel and communication failures, migration and protests are not attributable to one sanction alone.
- Venezuela's prior fragility: [[venezuelas-recent-economic-history-update]] identifies Chávez-era [[OilRevenueDependence|oil-spending]] dependence, fixed bolívar exchange rates from 2003, official dollar-import approvals (Alex Rosenberg's delays) and the 2014 oil-price fall before describing [[DonaldTrump|Trump]]-era restrictions on oil sales as a further hyperinflation catalyst. Alejandro Velasco's [[DollarizedStabilization|dollar-access]] account distinguishes partial stabilization via remittances from recovery for those outside the dollar economy.
- Bargaining and representation: [[caracas-under-pressure-democracy-in-venezuela-6a7d8bbe7d85cbdf96e00822]] says [[USDepartmentOfState|State Department]] approval for Venezuelan oil royalties/taxes gives [[MarcoRubio|Marco Rubio]] leverage in talks over Supreme Court and electoral reform under Delcy Rodríguez; María Corina Machado's exclusion from the table remains a legitimacy limit, even if she said she would judge outcomes. January 2026 political events are reported by the episode, not independently verified here.
- [[Afghanistan|Afghanistan]]'s limit case: [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]] reports Western nonrecognition, aid cuts, sanctions and frozen central-bank reserves alongside persistent [[TalibanHardlineRule|Taliban]] hardline restrictions on women's education/work and a health-care and livelihood crisis. Its correspondent's report advocates continued aid and sanctions reform; the episode also notes reduced street violence, which does not cancel material harm.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- The [[USIranNuclearDiplomacy|Iran nuclear deal]] shows potential coercive leverage; this moral argument is not a proof that every sanction fails or is literally warfare. Regime repression and domestic policy remain separate causes.
- Cuba's patron loss and pandemic, Venezuela's controls and oil cycle, and Afghanistan's Taliban choices confound single-cause attribution. The Caracas and Afghanistan episodes each spend substantial time on unrelated stories; only relevant segments support the synthesis.
- Episode-reported casualty/mortality estimates are not reproduced as independently verified statistics.

## What Changed
- Separated harm channels, diplomatic conditions and pre-existing vulnerabilities across three countries plus a bargaining case.
