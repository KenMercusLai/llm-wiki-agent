# Good Jobs For Non-College Workers

## Definition
Good jobs for non-college workers are jobs accessible without a four-year degree that provide sustainable compensation, conditions, progression and local opportunity; manufacturing is one possible channel, not the definition itself.

## Current Synthesis
The manufacturing and China-shock interviews ground the worker-versus-place distinction. A restaurant automation test, educational pathway proposal and detention-town case complicate what new jobs count as good; government and celebrity forecasts are arguments, not verified wage outcomes.

## Key Claims
- Manufacturing has historically paid a non-college premium, but wage and working conditions differ sharply across industries.
- Restoring activity to a place does not automatically restore the displaced workers' earnings or labor-force attachment.
- Automation can lower owner costs while changing skill premiums and creating different supporting tasks.
- Education and vocational pathways must be connected to real job access, not promises of aggregate industrial growth.
- A job's public purpose, worker conditions and policy dependence matter alongside pay and headcount.

## Evidence
- Wage quality, not factory symbolism: Planet Money reports research by David Card, Jesse Rothstein and Moises Yee estimating an average 35% earnings lift for a restaurant worker moving into manufacturing, with little premium in garments and more in autos, aircraft and petrochemicals. It quotes Gordon Hanson saying there are too few good jobs for non-college workers, compares reported 1.6 additional local jobs over ten years per manufacturing job with five for an average tech job in Enrico Moretti's estimate, and notes heat, repetitive work, low wages and rigid shifts in some factories. These are episode-reported estimates, not an all-sector wage guarantee. [[why-are-we-so-obsessed-with-manufacturing]]
- Worker versus place: David Autor describes the post-2001 China import shock as costing well over a million U.S. manufacturing jobs, concentrated in local labor markets; retail, warehouse, health or food-service jobs later returned to some places without making the originally displaced workers whole. The discussion favors direct adjustment aid and carefully paired sector investments over assuming blanket tariffs repair individual losses. [[why-economists-got-free-trade-with-china-so-wrong]]
- Task substitution: a Philadelphia Chinatown comparison reports [[PhuongHoangChiang|Phuong Hoang Chiang]]'s 13 years of wok experience, $35/hour pay and 20–30 dishes/hour, versus [[KennyPoon|Kenny Poon]]'s partially automated [[RobbyWokbot|Robby]] rental near $5/hour; chef Shola Olunloyo identified all three preparations, awarding two dishes to the human and fried rice to the robot. The episode invokes Daron Acemoglu's distinction between displacement and complementary new tasks; it is one taste/cost setting, not an economy-wide forecast. [[chef-vs-robot]]
- Access and training: Rahm Emanuel argues every high-school graduate should have a college, community-college, military or vocational route, beginning with reading proficiency. Carolyn Lee's manufacturing-workforce discussion links vacancies to skills, certificates and apprenticeship, while Oren Cass says employers facing scarcity should offer better pay. Lutnick's 2026 growth/tariff/TSMC narrative mentions a workforce pipeline but gives no verified high-wage trades outcome. [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-rahm-emanuel-trumps-foreign-policy-china-europes-decline-immigration-dsa-vs-democrats-42416310]] [[why-are-we-so-obsessed-with-manufacturing]] [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-howard-lutnick-how-america-can-hit-6-gdp-growth-in-2026-39668255]]
- Fragile and contested employment: the Folkston, Georgia ICE detention facility reportedly expanded from 1,100 beds to capacity for up to 3,000, adding about 200 jobs and roughly $1 million for county/city coffers under GEO Group's federal contract. Savannah Pollock and Glenn Hall question dependence on immigration policy and the human cost of detention; disputed ICE training-hour numbers do not decide the local-jobs question. Adam Carolla advocates electricians, plumbers, framers, roofers and drywallers for [[Malibu]], [[PacificPalisades|Pacific Palisades]] and [[Altadena]] fire rebuilding and predicts lower near-term AI exposure; these are his expectations, not measured labor-market gains. [[the-ice-hiring-boom]] [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-adam-carolla-on-californias-collapse-fires-failed-leadership-and-gyno-fascism-39710360]]

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Autor's reported findings do not prove every tariff or reshoring program fails, and construction or robotics examples cannot establish economy-wide displacement. Emanuel's note does not substantiate the old page's minimum-wage indexing or EITC specifics. Lutnick's growth, tariff and investment numbers in a friendly interview are unverified claims, not proof that accessible well-paid jobs appeared. Detention employment has ethical and election-dependent costs; reported ICE training hours conflict across sources cited by the episode.

## What Changed
- Shifted the criterion from factory job counts to worker outcomes and pathways.
- Removed unsupported specific wage-policy commitments and separated skilled-trades advocacy from observed jobs.
