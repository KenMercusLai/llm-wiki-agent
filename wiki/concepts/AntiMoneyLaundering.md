# Anti-Money Laundering

## Definition
Anti-money laundering (AML) is the set of risk-based identification, monitoring, reporting, and investigation practices used to detect and interrupt attempts to disguise criminal proceeds as legitimate funds. Related foreign-exchange, brokerage-licensing, and sanctions obligations can overlap with AML without every violation being proof of laundering.

## Current Synthesis
An illustrative placement–layering–integration model helps explain why an institution asks both who controls an account and whether later transactions fit the customer's stated activity. Individuals can unwittingly provide accounts or counterparties for suspicious flows; cross-border routes add questions of purpose and provenance. For a crypto product, partner-bank access and targeted enforcement also make controls part of operations rather than a post-launch formality. Controls must respect lawful data-sharing boundaries and distinguish illicit activity from legitimate payments.

## Key Claims
- **C1 — Detection follows the flow:** Identity and source-of-funds checks at entry are paired with continuing review of unusual transfers and transaction chains, not a one-time account-opening form.
- **C2 — Personal and cross-border exposure:** Account lending, opaque third-party transfers, and informal overseas-investment funding can expose users to investigation or misuse even when they do not see the whole chain.
- **C3 — Compliance enables products:** A bank partner may require a crypto startup to articulate AML controls before enabling core transfer features; the operator's account illustrates a dependency, not proof of effective controls.
- **C4 — Targeted virtual-asset enforcement:** Crypto rails can facilitate sanctions evasion and fraud networks, while tracing and legitimate uses support actor- and channel-specific responses rather than treating crypto activity as uniformly criminal.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- Overseas brokerage solicitation, misuse of personal foreign-exchange allowances, and sanctions evasion are related compliance questions; none alone establishes that the funds are proceeds of a predicate crime. The brokerage episode's current-rule details are episode assertions, not legal advice.
- The Coinbase episode is Armstrong's retrospective and does not independently audit the firm's control quality. Foreign-bank practices differ by institution, jurisdiction, and customer segment.
- The crypto figures and activity characterizations are attributed to TRM Labs and the Marketplace Tech interview, not independently verified totals. Traceability does not erase the difficulty of identifying beneficial owners, and lawful information-sharing limits constrain investigations.

## What Changed
- Reframes onboarding, ongoing review, consumer exposure, and product access as distinct parts of a single compliance system.
- Separates AML from adjacent securities, foreign-exchange, and sanctions obligations instead of treating every gray route as a proved laundering offense.
- Preserves legitimate crypto use and tracing alongside targeted network-enforcement concerns.
