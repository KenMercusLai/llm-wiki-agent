# Data Center Cost Shifting

## Definition
Data center cost shifting is the possibility that grid or local public costs created by large computing facilities are borne by households, small businesses or governments instead of the facility beneficiary.

## Current Synthesis
A new substation or generation contract can outlive the anticipated compute demand. [[PublicUtilityCommissions]] can assign upgrade costs through upfront payments and long contracts; separate tax exemptions, water, land and pollution burdens require other public decisions. Pledges to pay more are proposals or announcements, not measured proof that every household bill is protected.

## Key Claims
- Regulated rate design determines whether dedicated grid expansion falls on the large customer or a broader ratepayer pool.
- Direct payments, onsite supply and long-term commitments can reduce cross-subsidy, subject to actual utility contracts and monitoring.
- Tax concessions are a distinct public-finance transfer; promised construction, jobs and property-tax gains must be compared with foregone revenue.
- Electricity, water, noise and land burdens shape local consent and can delay or pause projects, with financing consequences.
- Household affordability connects this technical allocation dispute to AI's political legitimacy, but not every price rise is attributable to AI.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The Marketplace Tech items form a reporting cluster, not independent rate studies. Some projects may add supply or pay their own upgrade costs; general tariffs also reflect old infrastructure and wildfire damage. Microsoft pledges and politicians' promises require project-level contracts and realized bill evidence. A lost tax dollar, a higher utility rate and a local water burden are different channels. The announced New York pause and voters' choice are jurisdiction- and date-specific; financial causation in Michigan is not quantified.

## What Changed
- Distinguished regulated electricity rates from tax expenditures and site-level resource burdens.
- Put corporate promises and local resistance next to the missing empirical attribution of household rate increases.
