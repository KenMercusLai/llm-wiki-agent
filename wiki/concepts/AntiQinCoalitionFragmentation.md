# Anti-Qin Coalition Fragmentation / 反秦阵营诸侯化分裂

## Definition
Anti-Qin coalition fragmentation describes how a shared enemy did not create a unified rebel command: local forces restored old states, defended separate titles and territory, and sometimes fought one another while Qin remained militarily dangerous.

## Current Synthesis
The fracture begins with Zhao's decision to strengthen its own territory instead of following Zhang Chu west, then spreads across revived Qi, Yan, and Wei and within Chen Sheng's own army. Claims to royal succession, command legitimacy, and personal security shape whether actors cooperate, defect, attach to stronger armies, or refuse rescue. Chu's restored kingship and the Huaiwang reward rule attempt to coordinate the field, but Song Yi's command crisis and the watching lords at Julu show their limits. Xiang Yu's victory produces temporary deference to a victorious commander, not evidence that the coalition's conflicting interests had disappeared.

## Key Claims
- **C1 — Territorial restoration competes with common strategy:** New Zhao and other revived states turn anti-Qin mobilization into separate state-building and bargaining.
- **C2 — Command fractures from within:** False or disputed authorization, the killing or desertion of commanders, and Chen Sheng's weakened personal authority undermine even nominally unified rebel armies.
- **C3 — Title and succession disputes obstruct cooperation:** Local defections, rival Chu claimants, and Qi's succession conditions convert status conflicts into failed joint operations; attachment can also consolidate forces without every leader claiming kingship.
- **C4 — Royal legitimacy and reward rules are partial repairs:** The restored Chu royal line and Huaiwang's Guanzhong promise seek to coordinate armies, but royal appointment does not settle timing, command, or the obligation to rescue Zhao.
- **C5 — Victory recenters, rather than abolishes, fragmentation:** Xiang Yu's intervention at Julu draws submission from previously passive lords, shifting coordination toward his military authority.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- Fragmentation did not make anti-Qin cooperation impossible: Chen Ying and Ying Bu joined Xiang Liang, Mi Xin created coordinated assignments, and Xiang Yu assembled battlefield deference. The unity was contingent and did not prove a settled constitutional or interstate order.
- Song Yi's wait is presented mainly through its failed outcome and Xiang Yu's argument; the independent feasibility of letting Qin and Zhao exhaust each other is not established by these notes. Killing an appointed commander is itself a rupture, not an unqualified coordination success.
- The Qinji 132 title advertises Julu victory, but its text supplies the setup; the battle sequence belongs to Qinji 134. The host's inference that Chen Sheng approved Wu Guang's killing is not proved by his later promotion of Tian Zang, and the moral reading of Chen Sheng's collapse remains source-scoped.
- Several podcast transcripts have variant names and incomplete passages. The contrast between 《史记》 and 《资治通鉴》 over Zhang Er and Chen Yu's resentment makes blame for Zhao's separation historiographically sensitive; no single motive should be asserted as settled.

## What Changed
- Replaces a chronological accumulation of rebel episodes with five recurring mechanisms: separate territory, internal command, status rivalry, partial institutional repair, and victory-based recentering.
- Treats renewed attachment and royal coordination as countervailing responses, not proof that the fragmentation pattern was inevitable or permanent.
- Distinguishes reported events from host inferences, contested motives, and the Julu setup-versus-battle boundary.
